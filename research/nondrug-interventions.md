# Non-drug human interventions: evidence and limits

## Verdict

No intervention reviewed here has demonstrated the requested combination in healthy people: onset in minutes to hours, a fully reversible enhanced state, and large simultaneous improvements in learning, memory, reasoning and attention. This is a failure of demonstrated capability, not a proof of biological impossibility.

There are real partial leads. Brain stimulation can change particular cognitive operations; targeted stimulation can improve episodic memory; stimulation paired with learning can sometimes improve acquisition; and metabolic or environmental interventions can affect performance acutely. But the better-established effects are generally small or domain-specific. Some apparently large effects come from small studies, weak controls, clinical populations, or disputed meta-analytic calculations. Invasive memory prostheses are a particularly important proof of principle, but not an available enhancement route for a healthy undergraduate.

The strongest practical distinction is not “drug versus device.” It is a general enhancement of capacity versus a change in one task, an improvement in encoding selected material, restoration from fatigue or disease, or assistance from an external computer. None of those narrower outcomes should be relabeled broad improvement.

## Scope, verification and interpretation

Evidence checked on 4 October 2026. This is a targeted, citation-verified narrative evidence review, not a preregistered systematic review of every published experiment. Searches used Europe PMC through curl, including modality-specific meta-analyses, systematic reviews, replication/preregistration terms, landmark trials and subsequent corrections. Full texts were opened where Europe PMC supplied them; otherwise the indexed abstract and bibliographic record were opened. Every source below carries its actual verification level. No citation is presented as verified from memory alone.

Thematic coverage is complete across the requested intervention families. Literature enumeration, full-text assessment of every primary study, registry-to-publication matching, and rare or long-term adverse-event assessment are partial. Large preregistered replication evidence is unavailable in the sources located for several emerging routes; that absence is stated, not converted into either success or failure. Some full-text endpoints returned errors, and some abstracts omit numerical estimates. Those gaps are not filled with invented numbers. This review does not redo the separate headroom, acute-mechanism or dry-lab-discovery reviews.

Hedges' g, Cohen's d and standardized mean difference (SMD) express differences relative to study-specific variability. They are not percentage increases in intelligence and cannot be added across interventions. Different meta-analyses use different tasks, populations and controls, so their numbers are not a head-to-head leaderboard. A pooled effect across different domains in different experiments does not demonstrate simultaneous broad enhancement in the same people. Likewise, a change in an EEG rhythm, blood biomarker, blood flow or decoding accuracy is not automatically a cognitive benefit.

“Reversible” is treated as return of the enhancement-producing physiological state to baseline, not erasure of memories learned during that state. Switching a device off establishes termination of input, not necessarily termination of after-effects or complete biological reversibility. The available studies rarely establish the latter rigorously. Retaining something learned is not itself an adverse effect, but sustained training-related changes are not evidence of an on-demand reversible cognitive state.

Metabolic and endocrine interventions cross the non-drug boundary. Diet, exercise, sleep and stress regulation belong directly here. Exogenous ketones and creatine are included as nutritional/metabolic evidence, not as a claim that ingestion is conceptually different from pharmacology. Hormone-administration trials are used only to test endocrine enhancement claims; administering a hormone remains a drug intervention. No doses, device settings, sourcing, synthesis or self-experimentation procedures are proposed.

## 1. Electrical and magnetic brain stimulation

### tDCS: small possible benefits, not a dependable cognitive switch

Transcranial direct current stimulation changes excitability and the conditions under which task-related plasticity occurs; it does not supply knowledge. The historical literature is genuinely mixed. Horvath and colleagues' quantitative review found no significant effect in any of 59 analyses of single-session cognitive outcomes in healthy adults [1]. That should not be treated as a permanent verdict against all later protocols: a 2026 preregistered meta-analysis of electrical stimulation and attention included 58 trials and reported SMD 0.24, 95% CI 0.12–0.36. The prefrontal anodal-tDCS subgroup had SMD 0.26, 95% CI 0.12–0.39 [2]. These are small attentional effects, not a broad large enhancement.

Pairing stimulation with practice does not solve the transfer problem. A 2026 robust-variance meta-analysis of 23 studies of healthy adults compared active tDCS plus cognitive training with sham plus the same training. Its pooled working-memory benefit was g = 0.191, 95% CI 0.062–0.320. Trained-task benefits were significant; untrained working-memory benefits were smaller and nonsignificant, although the direct difference between those categories was itself nonsignificant [3]. Thus it is wrong both to advertise proven general transfer and to claim this analysis proved that transfer is impossible.

Onset can fall within the requested minutes-to-hours window, during or shortly after a session. Repeated-training studies operate over days or weeks. Persistence and individual response vary; an acute benefit does not establish a reliable on/off state, and learning-related after-effects may last beyond stimulation.

Replication audit: Arar and colleagues conducted a double-blind, preregistered trial in 150 younger and 91 cognitively normal older adults. It directly tested earlier claims of improved memory retrieval and examined time of day, task difficulty and stimulus format. It found no effect on episodic or working memory [4]. This is an especially relevant large confirmatory failure, not merely an underpowered pilot null. It does not invalidate every stimulation target, but it substantially weakens the proposition that ordinary prefrontal tDCS reliably boosts memory in healthy people.

Profile assessment: timing sometimes fits; large magnitude and broad transfer do not. General-purpose single-session tDCS is a poor lead for the stated goal. Small task-specific adjunctive benefits remain credible research questions.

### tACS: selective timing control, modest average gains

Transcranial alternating current stimulation attempts to influence oscillatory timing and communication between networks. Lee and colleagues' healthy-young-adult meta-analysis included 56 studies and 1,797 participants. Cognitive-performance outcomes yielded SMD 0.161, 95% CI 0.109–0.214; reaction-time outcomes were nonsignificant, SMD 0.062, 95% CI −0.055–0.180. The performance analysis also showed funnel asymmetry and a significant Egger test, so small-study/publication bias remains relevant [5].

Grover and colleagues' broader 2023 synthesis included 102 studies and 2,893 people across healthy, aging and neuropsychiatric populations. It reported modest-to-moderate benefits across several domains, including working memory, long-term memory, attention, executive control and fluid intelligence [6]. That provides a wider positive signal, but not evidence that one intervention broadly improves every domain in a healthy undergraduate. The accessible abstract did not give its pooled numerical estimate, so the numerical anchor here is the directly applicable healthy-young-adult analysis.

Headline studies matter, with their boundaries intact. Reinhart and Nguyen reported rapid improvement in older adults' working memory after stimulation, lasting beyond a 50-minute post-stimulation observation period [7]. Grover and colleagues later reported dissociable auditory-verbal working- and long-term-memory improvements after four days of stimulation in older adults, with benefits still evident a month later and larger gains among those with lower baseline cognition [8]. These are aging-memory results, not instantaneous universal intelligence gains. The latter protocol also does not meet the requested acute onset as a complete intervention.

Replication audit: a 2026 preregistered study tested the claim that slowing theta rhythms expands working-memory capacity. It found neither behavioral improvement nor the predicted EEG modulation, regardless of task difficulty or baseline capacity [9]. This is a protocol-level negative test, not a large multisite refutation of the entire field, and the opened abstract did not report sample size. No large preregistered independent replication establishing the famous broad interpretation of the aging-memory headlines was located. That status is “not established,” not “replicated” or “disproved.”

Profile assessment: one of the closer noninvasive state-control concepts, but the most relevant pooled estimate is small and effects are selective. Full reversibility is not demonstrated merely by stopping current; durable plasticity is sometimes the desired outcome.

For completeness, transcranial random-noise stimulation does not currently rescue the electrical approach. A 2026 meta-analysis of 13 RCTs and 403 healthy adults reported g = 0.17, 95% CI −0.06–0.40. A positive online-stimulation subgroup did not produce a significant between-subgroup difference [10]. It is an uncertain adjunct, not a hidden large-effect alternative.

### TMS: stronger causal perturbation, still selective enhancement

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation can directly perturb cortical activity and connected networks. In 53 studies involving 1,507 healthy participants, offline excitatory stimulation produced small improvements in accuracy, g = 0.12, and reaction time, g = −0.13. Executive-function accuracy was g = 0.14 [11]. These broad averages are nowhere near a dramatic change in capability.

Network-targeted episodic-memory stimulation is a more interesting exception to that weak average. A 2026 meta-analysis of hippocampal-indirectly-targeted stimulation included 38 quantitatively usable studies and 1,009 participants across healthy and clinical populations. Episodic memory improved by g = 0.44, 95% CI 0.34–0.54; removing outliers reduced this to g = 0.38, 95% CI 0.29–0.46. Other cognitive domains were essentially unaffected: g = 0.04, 95% CI −0.01–0.09 [12]. Its selectivity is evidence for a real network-specific effect and direct evidence against interpreting it as generalized enhancement.

The same paper reported a model-projected g = 0.66 for a favorable combination of task and timing characteristics. That is a meta-regression projection, not an independently confirmed optimized treatment. The included designs mix controlled and uncontrolled comparisons; average samples contributing memory effects were small. Importantly, its full text reports funnel asymmetry and a significant Egger test while arguing that publication bias is unlikely to explain the result. Sensitivity to outliers cannot by itself settle publication bias. The review also describes a failure-to-replicate study followed by a favorable reanalysis; this is a contested replication history, not a clean large preregistered success [12].

Some TMS effects occur in a session; other memory studies use repeated sessions over days or weeks. Physiological after-effects and learned memories can outlast stimulation. Treatment success in depression is not evidence of above-baseline cognition in healthy people. TMS also requires specialized equipment, screening and supervision; “noninvasive” is not equivalent to risk-free or completely reversible.

Profile assessment: among the better-supported partial leads for a meaningful memory improvement, but broad reasoning and attention gains are specifically missing. It is not an accessible general cognitive switch for Rolf without a collaborating laboratory.

### Temporal interference: include the deep-targeting lead, without promoting it prematurely

Temporal interference uses interacting electrical fields to attempt noninvasive modulation of deep structures. Violante and colleagues demonstrated hippocampal targeting with modeling and human experiments [13]. Their first, 20-person imaging experiment changed physiology without improving memory accuracy; a separate 21-person behavioral experiment found a small improvement in recall accuracy during stimulation. Overall accuracy at the later retest did not differ, although an analysis restricted to items initially recalled correctly favored stimulation. This is relevant because it could extend electrical stimulation beyond accessible superficial cortex, but the behavioral finding is substantially narrower than a general memory upgrade.

The opened human proof-of-concept does not establish a broad effect, a healthy-cognition meta-analytic magnitude, or full reversibility. No large independent preregistered replication of broadly improved behavior was located. Onset can be acute; long-term cognitive and safety generalization remain unsettled. This is an emerging deep-network tool, not a validated substitute for invasive stimulation.

## 2. Focused ultrasound

Low-intensity focused ultrasound potentially reaches deeper targets than conventional surface stimulation. It must be distinguished from high-intensity ablative ultrasound and from ultrasound-assisted blood–brain-barrier opening. The latter interventions cannot be used as evidence for an innocuous reversible enhancement device.

A particularly relevant 2026 double-blind, sham-controlled within-person study targeted bilateral centromedian thalamus in 16 healthy adults. It reported faster reaction times on a directed-attention task lasting at least 40 minutes after stimulation. Different stimulation conditions could have different directions of effect; pre/post MRI detected no structural neuroradiological changes [14]. This is an acute human signal in an arousal-related deep circuit, but the abstract provides no standardized behavioral effect size, and normal MRI in a small experiment does not establish complete biological reversibility.

No mature meta-analysis establishing a healthy-human, broad cognitive enhancement magnitude was located. Even in Alzheimer's disease, a 2026 synthesis of three randomized trials, N = 84, found no significant pooled global-cognition benefit: SMD 0.31, p = 0.54. Its modality subgroups were exploratory and should not be exported to healthy people [16].

Replication audit: auditory and somatic confounding are concrete, demonstrated problems, not generic methodological objections. Braun and colleagues showed that healthy participants could hear ultrasound stimulation and distinguish active from inactive trials; appropriate auditory masking removed the associated auditory EEG signal and reduced detection to chance [15]. A change in attention could therefore reflect sensory alerting or imperfect blinding unless those alternatives are controlled. No large preregistered independent replication of the thalamic attention result was located.

Profile assessment: high research interest because of acute deep-network access, but currently very low certainty about enhancement magnitude and breadth. It is not a dead mechanism; the claimed capability remains unestablished and requires a specialist research setting.

## 3. Vagus nerve stimulation paired with learning

Vagal afferent stimulation is intended to recruit ascending neuromodulatory systems, making task-related events more effective at driving plasticity. The important claim is temporally paired learning enhancement, not simply feeling more alert during ear or neck stimulation. Implanted, auricular and cervical stimulation are not interchangeable interventions.

The 2021 healthy-person meta-analysis of 19 studies reported a weighted cognitive effect of 0.21 [17]. A larger 2026 review included 53 studies, with 30 quantitatively analyzed and over 1,500 participants. It reported overall g = 0.41, 95% CI 0.30–0.53; executive functions g = 0.46; flexibility/learning g = 0.53; and working memory/attention g = 0.19. Its full text found evidence of publication bias; trim-and-fill reduced the overall estimate to g = 0.338, 95% CI 0.220–0.455 [18]. The newer estimate mixes healthy and clinical samples. A nonsignificant moderator for population does not prove equivalence between them.

Llanos and colleagues' speech-learning experiment is a useful paired-stimulation demonstration. In 24 native English speakers, stimulation paired with easier-to-learn Mandarin tone categories improved category learning and retention, including generalization to new exemplars. Pairing with harder categories did not produce the same benefit, and EEG did not show the predicted change in sensory representations [19]. This is selective perceptual/category learning, not acquisition of a language or broad memory expansion.

Effects can emerge during a training session; retained learning may persist. Stopping stimulation does not undo learned material or prove that the induced plasticity state immediately returns to baseline. Noninvasive systems avoid implantation but still face uncertainty about whether the intended nerve and central pathway were engaged.

Replication audit: a registered 2025 cervical-VNS study explicitly attempted to replicate enhanced learning of visual radar targets. Thirty-four participants completed training and delayed assessments; active stimulation did not improve learning, vigilance or response inhibition [20]. It is not a large replication and its protocol differs from auricular paired speech training. Nevertheless, the earlier benefit did not generalize to this implementation. Explanations involving imperfect target engagement remain hypotheses, not evidence that an effective treatment has already been identified.

There is stronger confirmatory evidence for implanted paired VNS in rehabilitation, but not for the requested cognition profile. The registered VNS-REHAB trial randomized 108 stroke survivors across 19 services. After six weeks, the between-group upper-limb impairment improvement was 2.6 Fugl-Meyer points, 95% CI 1.0–4.2 [21]. This is a positive human paired-stimulation result that must be preserved in the assessment, but it concerns recovery of motor function after stroke, not acute healthy cognition. Implantation also fails whole-intervention reversibility.

Profile assessment: a plausible learning adjunct with some positive pooled evidence, but no large broad acute gain. Task selection and pairing are central, and rigorous independent replication of healthy cognitive enhancement remains inadequate.

## 4. Closed-loop sleep stimulation and targeted memory reactivation

These approaches target offline consolidation, not an awake enhanced state. They can act over a nap or night, so some meet an hours-scale timescale, but they cannot be assumed to compress all needed sleep or permit reliable new factual learning while asleep.

For acoustic slow-wave enhancement, Wunderlin and colleagues pooled 10 studies, 11 experiments and 177 healthy participants. The overall memory effect was g = 0.25, p = 0.07; phase-locked stimulation yielded g = 0.36, p = 0.047; and a young-adult phase-locked subgroup yielded g = 0.44, p = 0.01 [22]. The overall result was nonsignificant and subgroup findings should not be sold as a general product effect.

A major correction changes the evidence base: the Stanyer acoustic-stimulation meta-analysis was retracted in 2025. The retraction states that standard errors were used instead of standard deviations and sample sizes were reported incorrectly; its results and conclusions were considered invalid [23]. Its attractive effect estimates should not be recycled as supporting evidence.

Direct replication also separates physiology from behavior. Henin and colleagues increased slow oscillations and spindle activity but found no memory benefit in either a 12-person nap/navigation experiment or a 19-person double-blind nocturnal word-pair replication [24]. These are small, not large preregistered trials. They nevertheless show why “the device increased deep-sleep oscillations” is not an adequate cognitive endpoint. No large preregistered independent trial closing the general enhancement claim was located.

Targeted memory reactivation is distinct: previously learned material is associated with cues, and those cues are presented during sleep. A meta-analysis of 91 experiments and 2,004 participants found g = 0.29, 95% CI 0.21–0.38. Benefits occurred in non-REM sleep; the analyzed REM and waking conditions did not show significant effects [25]. This is one of the clearer modest consolidation effects, but it strengthens selected prior learning rather than installing knowledge or raising general reasoning capacity.

Reversibility concerns include possible sleep disruption and unknown repeated-use effects; turning off sound is easy, but resulting consolidation is not an on/off cognitive state. The target is a biological sleep process, and more externally driven oscillation is not necessarily better sleep.

Profile assessment: useful research on a narrow memory component, not a strong candidate for broad acute enhancement. Restoration of adequate sleep and sleep-disorder treatment must be kept separate from enhancement beyond a well-rested healthy baseline.

## 5. Neurofeedback

EEG, fNIRS and real-time fMRI neurofeedback train people to alter a measured signal. A successful feedback task can reflect genuine self-regulation, a learned strategy, expectancy, relaxation or ordinary repetition. Evidence must therefore distinguish signal modulation from improved untrained cognition and compare with credible sham feedback.

Kimura and colleagues identified 41 randomized trials qualitatively and 15 trials with 569 healthy adults quantitatively. Attention improved overall by SMD 0.27, 95% CI 0.10–0.44, but the eight trials comparing neurofeedback with sham feedback had no significant pooled effect [26]. Jackson and colleagues' episodic-memory synthesis found g = 0.31 in active-controlled studies, but mixed healthy and clinical populations and highlighted underpowered, heterogeneous designs [27]. Thus it would be too strong to claim all cognitive neurofeedback effects are placebo; it is also unsupported to promise large general benefits from a consumer headband.

Onset is usually after repeated sessions rather than a switch applied once; some physiological changes can occur within a session. Learned self-regulation or strategy changes can persist, with limited long-term follow-up. There is no established universal washout period.

Replication audit: a 2026 preregistered double-blind experiment compared genuine feedback, n = 30, with sham, n = 30, and passive control, n = 32. Alpha power increased across groups independently of feedback authenticity or active self-regulation [28]. This is a specific failure of the proposed single-session mechanism, not a definitive test of every multi-session protocol. Together with the sham-controlled meta-analytic result, it substantially weakens headline claims based on within-group EEG improvements.

Profile assessment: weak as an acute large-effect route. Laboratory self-regulation research remains legitimate; consumer “brain optimization” claims exceed the demonstrated transfer.

## 6. Brain–computer interfaces and memory prostheses

Three technologies need to stay separate. A passive BCI estimates cognitive state. An assistive BCI uses neural signals to control an external device. A stimulation-based memory prosthesis tries to change biological encoding itself. Better device control or better state classification is not evidence of increased intrinsic intelligence.

The strongest enhancement-like evidence comes from invasive systems in clinical participants. Hampson and colleagues' hippocampal modeling/stimulation proof of concept reported 37% improvement on its short-term/working-memory task and 35% improvement in retention of visual information [29]. These are the paper's task-specific reported percentages, not percentage increases in general memory capacity or IQ. They are not a healthy-person meta-analytic estimate.

Ezzyat and colleagues used recordings from 25 patients undergoing epilepsy monitoring to identify poor encoding states and trigger stimulation. Their full text reports a 15% relative increase in item-recall probability with lateral temporal stimulation [30]. This establishes that state-dependent stimulation can sometimes rescue encoding failures; it does not establish a new broad high-performance state.

Later work is not uniformly positive. Roeder and colleagues' content-sensitive hippocampal prosthesis study found both increases and decreases in memory performance; benefits were concentrated in memory-impaired patients receiving bilateral stimulation [31]. Direct medial temporal stimulation has also impaired spatial and temporal memory in humans [32]. Site, timing and neural code matter enough that “more stimulation” is not a meaningful strategy.

Noninvasive passive BCI evidence is much weaker as an enhancement claim. A 2025 fNIRS prototype reported 71% cross-validated discrimination of task states [33]. That is classification performance, not a demonstrated memory improvement. External memory aids or computer assistance could increase effective task performance, but would constitute augmentation by a tool rather than proof of broad biological enhancement.

Onset can be trial-by-trial after calibration, but recording, training and surgery are substantial prerequisites. Electrical input can stop; implantation, surgical risks and tissue responses are not fully reversible. A healthy-human cognitive meta-analysis or large preregistered independent replication was not located.

Profile assessment: important causal proof of principle for targeted memory engineering; wrong population, wrong accessibility, narrow effect, and failure of the fully reversible whole-intervention requirement. Not a realistic route for a healthy undergraduate without clinical infrastructure.

## 7. Light and photobiomodulation

### Retinal light exposure

Light affects circadian timing and alertness through retinal pathways. A meta-analysis of 29 studies with 1,210 healthy participants reported improvement in subjective alertness, SMD −0.28, 95% CI −0.49 to −0.06, and objective alertness, SMD −0.34, 95% CI −0.68 to −0.01. Negative values indicate better outcomes on the measures used [34]. This is a modest alerting effect, not proof of improved learning, reasoning and memory together.

Onset can be within exposure, fitting the acute window. Circadian effects may extend after the light is removed, so switching off illumination is not a complete reversibility test. Time of day, prior sleep and prior light exposure affect interpretation; a night-time fatigue countermeasure is not automatically an enhancement of a fully rested daytime baseline.

Replication audit: the pooled signal exists, but no large preregistered independent replication of broad cognitive enhancement was located. Alertness and sleepiness are the supported endpoints. Profile assessment: a credible small state-management tool, not a large-capacity intervention.

### Transcranial photobiomodulation

Red/near-infrared photobiomodulation is proposed to alter mitochondrial and vascular processes. The hypothesis is not equivalent to demonstrated intracellular target engagement at every device's actual brain exposure, and retinal alerting should not be conflated with a transcranial metabolic effect.

A 2025 meta-analysis of 24 randomized trials and 820 participants reported substantial pooled effects: global cognition SMD 0.66, 95% CI 0.23–1.08; working-memory span SMD 1.41, 95% CI 0.78–2.04; attention SMD −1.15, 95% CI −2.05 to −0.24; and executive-function reaction time SMD −1.10, 95% CI −1.86 to −0.33 [35]. These are real published numbers and should not be softened away. They also pool healthy and cognitively impaired people across different endpoints and protocols. The healthy executive-reaction-time subgroup was nonsignificant. The authors themselves emphasize heterogeneity and study limitations.

Some constituent interventions are acute, others repeated; the meta-analysis cannot be read as the effect of one acute healthy-person protocol. Complete washout and long-term reversibility are not established. The opened abstract does not allow an independent numerical reconstruction of healthy-only effects across all domains.

Replication audit: no large preregistered independent replication confirming these large healthy-person cognitive effects was located. This is an evidence gap, not a documented failed replication. Small pooled samples spread across multiple outcomes, protocol diversity and uncertain generalization make photobiomodulation a low-certainty lead despite the appealing magnitude.

Profile assessment: one of the more striking published magnitude signals, but not a validated broad enhancer. It deserves scrutiny rather than dismissal or product-level confidence.

## 8. Exercise and its molecular mediators

Acute exercise has a much smaller cognitive effect than many summaries imply. Garrett and colleagues' Bayesian synthesis of 113 studies, 4,390 healthy young adults and 651 effect sizes reported g = 0.13 ± 0.04, with benefits particularly in reaction time and some executive tasks [36]. That is directly relevant to Rolf's population and the acute window, but it is not large.

Longer-term evidence must be presented with the disagreement intact. Ciria and colleagues' 2023 umbrella review of healthy-population randomized trials found d = 0.22, 95% CI 0.16–0.28; adjustment for active controls and baseline differences reduced it to d = 0.13, and publication-bias adjustment to d = 0.05, 95% CI −0.09–0.14 [37]. In contrast, Singh and colleagues' 2025 umbrella review spanning all ages and health states reported general cognition SMD 0.42, memory 0.26 and executive function 0.24, with positive results remaining after exclusion of low-quality reviews [38]. Differences in populations, included evidence, analytic choices and bias handling prevent treating either as a direct replacement for the other. The newer review's full text also reports a smaller general-cognition effect, d = 0.23, when restricted to high-quality reviews [38]. The larger all-population estimate is not the expected acute effect in a healthy young adult.

Replication audit: Lenze and colleagues' registered, two-site randomized trial enrolled 585 older adults with subjective cognitive concerns but no dementia. Exercise, mindfulness and their combination did not significantly improve primary memory or executive-function outcomes compared with the relevant controls at six months, nor at the secondary 18-month endpoint [39]. This is a substantial prospective test of lifestyle-related cognitive claims, not a test of acute exercise in undergraduates. It also provides no evidence that stacking those two interventions produces a special cognitive synergy.

Mechanisms are biologically interesting but should not substitute for behavior. A meta-analysis of repeated exercise found increased resting peripheral BDNF, SMD 0.39, 95% CI 0.17–0.60 [40]. That is a blood-biomarker effect, not a cognitive effect size. Proposed mediators include BDNF, lactate, ketone bodies, muscle-derived irisin and cathepsin B, vascular adaptation and inflammatory regulation. Much of the causal pathway evidence comes from animals or disease models; a human-oriented review explicitly called for more human evidence [41]. Peripheral BDNF does not quantify brain-wide plasticity, and no verified human intervention evidence here shows that changing one of these mediators alone produces a large broad acute enhancement.

Acute arousal/performance effects occur around a session and are transient. Fitness, vascular and learning adaptations accrue over weeks or longer and are not instantly reversible. These timescales should not be blended into one story of immediate brain rebuilding.

Profile assessment: credible health value and possible small acute cognitive benefit, but not close in magnitude. Molecular mediators are research leads, not evidence that exercise hides a broad-improvement response.

## 9. Ketones, fasting and other metabolic routes

### Ketones

Ketones supply an alternative cerebral fuel and participate in signaling. Their relevance is clearest when ordinary metabolism is compromised, but there are also healthy-person studies; it would be incorrect to dismiss the whole field as dementia-only.

A 2026 meta-analysis included 38 studies qualitatively, with 29 protocols and 1,117 participants quantitatively. Overall cognition improved by SMD 0.29, 95% CI 0.16–0.41. Its full text reports a nominally healthy subgroup SMD 0.36, 95% CI 0.18–0.54, but overall heterogeneity was very high, I² = 91% [42]. “Healthy” does not here mean a uniform rested undergraduate sample: ages, stressors, formulations and durations differ. The review's summary tables also contain inconsistent health-status labeling for some clinical studies, making the subgroup less clean than its label implies. It cannot establish one general-purpose fuel effect. Several authors disclosed ketone-product equity, patent or employment interests [42]; these do not invalidate the findings, but independent confirmation is particularly valuable.

An acute crossover study in 40 young adults reported metabolic changes and improved Stroop interference, assessed within the same session [43]. That supports an acute endpoint but does not demonstrate broad cognition or a large independently replicated effect. Conversely, a registered 12-week healthy-older-adult pilot with 30 randomized participants found no significant differences in exploratory functional, cognitive or quality-of-life outcomes [44]. Its null is not definitive because the outcomes were exploratory and the trial small.

Replication audit: the larger NOURISH AD trial randomized 413 people with mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's disease. The ketogenic caprylic-triglyceride formulation failed its prespecified cognitive endpoint and secondary measures [45]. The investigators noted possible inadequate ketone generation and lack of decline in placebo participants. This was a clinically important failure of one product strategy, not a refutation of all ketone physiology or a healthy-person replication.

Exogenous ketosis can arise in the acute window; dietary adaptation is slower. Blood fuel changes can subside, but the studies do not establish complete reversibility of any putative broad enhanced state. Ketogenic diets, fasting and individual ketone formulations are not interchangeable. No large preregistered healthy-person trial showing broad acute gains was located.

Profile assessment: an active modest-effect metabolic lead, not a demonstrated fuel-limited route to dramatically greater healthy cognition.

### Fasting, energy buffering and basic metabolic restoration

Fasting itself is not supported as an acute cognitive amplifier. A 2025 meta-analysis of 222 effect sizes and 3,484 healthy participants reported g = 0.02, 95% credible interval −0.05–0.10, comparing fasted with satiated states. Longer fasts and younger age were associated with modest reductions rather than larger enhancement [46]. This does not decide every long-term metabolic-health question; it does directly contradict the claim of a robust acute fasting-induced cognitive boost.

Creatine is relevant as an energy-buffering hypothesis, but its evidence requires a statistical audit. A 2024 meta-analysis reported memory SMD 0.31, 95% CI 0.18–0.44, alongside some timing benefits, with no significant overall-cognition or executive-function improvement [47]. Its 2025 corrigendum clarified that attention and processing-speed score outcomes were nonsignificant, distinct from response-time outcomes [48]. A 2026 methodological commentary identified non-independent outcomes being treated as independent and noted a previous creatine-memory reanalysis in which the overall effect lost significance except in older adults [49]. The commentary is a critique, not a new large preregistered trial. These disputed pooled estimates are not firm evidence of broad enhancement.

No acute, fully reversible, broad creatine-like metabolic enhancement state is established by these sources. Correcting inadequate nutrition, dehydration, hypoglycemia or disease may restore performance; that is not evidence that excess fuel improves a normally supplied healthy brain. Those restorative clinical questions are not comprehensively reviewed here, and no general glucose-enhancement effect size is asserted.

## 10. Endocrine routes

There is no separate evidence-supported category of “non-drug hormonal broad improvement.” Exercise, sleep, stress and circadian interventions affect endocrine signaling, but their cognition data are already evaluated above. Exogenous hormone trials help test the underlying claim; they do not become non-drug interventions because the hormone is natural.

Testosterone has unusually informative negative evidence. A meta-analysis of 23 randomized trials found overall cognitive g = 0.09, 95% CI −0.02–0.19, approaching g = 0.04 after publication-bias adjustment, with no significant benefit in individual cognitive domains [50]. The large registered Testosterone Trials cognitive study included 788 older men, with 493 meeting age-associated-memory-impairment criteria. After one year, the primary memory difference was −0.07 points, 95% CI −0.92–0.79, and other cognitive domains were not significantly improved [51]. These are not healthy-young-person trials, but they substantially weaken simple “raise testosterone, raise cognition” claims even in a population selected for low levels.

Growth hormone and IGF-related reasoning must separate deficiency correction from enhancement. A meta-analysis in GH-deficient adults found impairments relative to controls and moderate improvement relative to patients' own baselines during replacement [52]. Its abstract did not supply a single pooled treatment estimate; the numerical −0.46 to −1.46 range describes deficits, not the gain from treatment. It supplies no basis for large acute effects in hormonally normal healthy adults.

Thyroid normalization is similarly not a general enhancer. A double-blind randomized trial in 94 older people with subclinical hypothyroidism found no cognitive benefit from replacement at six or twelve months [53]. This does not imply that overt thyroid disease is cognitively irrelevant; it shows why more subtle biochemical changes cannot be assumed to improve cognition.

Insulin signaling is mechanistically relevant to cognition and metabolism, but a meta-analysis of 29 intranasal-insulin studies, pooled N = 1,726, found the positive global-cognition signal in AD/MCI, SMD 0.22, 95% CI 0.05–0.38. Global cognition was not significantly improved in healthy participants [54]. Again, this is pharmacological evidence about an endocrine mechanism, not a proposed non-drug protocol.

Stress manipulation can act within minutes, but is not broadly beneficial. Meta-analyses found impaired working memory and cognitive flexibility after acute stress, and a stage-dependent memory pattern: post-encoding stress can strengthen later retention, while stress around retrieval generally impairs it [55,56]. The episodic-memory synthesis included 113 studies and 6,216 participants. Its accessible abstract did not report a single pooled domain-independent effect size, and such a number would conceal the central bidirectionality. Cortisol elevation alone did not explain the whole behavioral response. Inducing a stronger hormonal response is not equivalent to enhancing cognition.

Sex-hormone claims also do not generalize into a cognitive switch: a 2026 review of randomized menopausal-hormone trials in younger menopausal women found no demonstrated cognitive benefit [71]. This concerns a different population, not Rolf's immediate options.

Onset ranges from rapid stress responses to months-long replacement studies. None establishes a large broad acute benefit in healthy hormonally normal people, and hormone clearance is not proof that all downstream physiological effects are fully reversible. Beyond the testosterone confirmatory trials, large preregistered healthy-enhancement replications were not located. Profile assessment: endocrine optimization is a dead end as a currently supported general healthy enhancement claim; diagnosis and treatment of an actual endocrine disorder is a separate question.

## 11. Hypoxia, hyperoxia, heat and cold

### Hypoxia and intermittent hypoxic conditioning

Acute oxygen deprivation is primarily an impairment risk, not a cognitive shortcut. A 2025 multilevel meta-analysis of 59 high-altitude-hypoxia studies and 739 effect sizes found overall g = −0.424. Impairment spanned several domains and depended on exposure and altitude [57]. This is not a direct test of carefully controlled intermittent conditioning, but it establishes why hypoxia cannot be described as generally brain-enhancing.

Repeated intermittent conditioning is a different hypothesis involving adaptive responses. A systematic review of sustained/repeated moderate hypoxia identified eight human studies with 274 participants; all were judged at high risk of bias because of limitations including randomization and assessor blinding [58]. A separate older-adult review identified seven studies and five registered trials, reporting some cognitive/cerebrovascular benefits but no significant BDNF effects for the examined training approaches [59]. Neither provides a dependable healthy-young-adult cognitive enhancement magnitude.

The proposed adaptation takes repeated exposure rather than minutes-to-hours delivery of a general benefit. Large preregistered healthy-person cognitive replication was not located. Reoxygenation can terminate exposure but cannot guarantee reversibility of injury. Profile assessment: acute hypoxia is a bad-direction lead; controlled conditioning is a low-certainty rehabilitation/adaptation question with the wrong timescale.

### Hyperoxia: a closer-looking signal that should not be overlooked

More oxygen is not simply the inverse of hypoxia, but it has an unusually relevant positive literature. A 2023 meta-analysis of acute normobaric hyperoxia in healthy humans included 23 studies and 76 effect estimates. It reported memory accuracy g = 0.34, memory speed g = 0.59, attention accuracy g = 0.59, attention speed g = 0.51, reaction speed g = 0.82, crystallized intelligence g = 0.73, executive function g = 0.88 and information processing g = 0.62 [60]. Those are among the largest and broadest reported acute non-drug-domain estimates in this review.

The same review rated overall evidence quality low, with an average Rosendal score of 47%, substantial heterogeneity and prediction intervals often crossing zero. Domains were supported by relatively few estimates, and a collection of improvements across studies is still not simultaneous broad enhancement in the same people. These estimates deserve direct confirmatory testing; they should not be quietly discarded, but neither do they establish broad-improvement capability. No large preregistered independent trial confirming that broad profile was located.

Input and blood-gas changes are acute, but complete reversibility and repeated-use safety are not established by cognitive test improvements. A separate hemodynamic meta-analysis found that hyperoxia could reduce cardiac output and increase vascular resistance, without evidence for increased systemic oxygen delivery [72]. Normobaric studies do not validate hyperbaric treatment packages, which introduce different exposure and logistical conditions.

Profile assessment: a relatively close fit to timing and reported cross-study breadth, but low-confidence magnitude and no decisive broad replication. A scientific lead, not a self-experimentation recommendation.

### Heat, sauna and cold

Thermal exposure reliably changes bodily state; that is not evidence of a beneficial cognitive state. A meta-analysis of thermal stressors included 49 publications and 528 effect sizes and found negative performance effects for both heat and cold. Cognitive tasks were less affected than psychomotor and perceptual tasks, but not established as enhanced [61]. The accessible abstract did not report a usable single pooled numerical effect size; none is invented here.

For cold specifically, a systematic review of 18 healthy-person experiments found cognitive impairment in 15, including attention/processing speed, executive function and memory, sometimes before hypothermia [62]. Feeling aroused after cold exposure is therefore not a reliable proxy for better cognition.

Sauna-dementia findings address a different question. The Finnish prospective cohort of 2,315 men associated more frequent sauna use with lower subsequent dementia incidence over a median 20.7-year follow-up [63]. It was observational, not random allocation to an acute cognitive enhancer. Selection, correlated lifestyle factors and residual confounding remain possible. Proposed heat-shock, vascular and endocrine mechanisms do not establish a large immediate behavioral gain.

Onset of heat/cold stress is rapid; any acclimation benefit requires repeated exposure. Restoration of comfortable temperature can reverse some performance impairment, but extreme thermal injury is not assuredly reversible. No large preregistered independent trial establishing broad acute enhancement from sauna, heat or cold was located. Profile assessment: dead ends as supported acute enhancement strategies, while long-term health/adaptation questions remain separate.

## 12. Training, expertise, mnemonic strategies and mindfulness

Training can generate very large differences in the skill actually practiced. The unsupported leap is to treat that as a large increase in general cognitive capacity.

Melby-Lervåg and colleagues' meta-analysis covered 87 publications and 145 experimental comparisons. Working-memory-related improvements did not convincingly transfer to intelligence, reading or arithmetic when compared with treated controls [64]. In a separate multilevel meta-analysis of typically developing children, far-transfer effects with active controls were essentially zero, g = 0.001, SE = 0.055 [65]. The latter is not an adult effect estimate, but it reinforces the distinction between learning a task and raising general ability.

Replication audit has both large-scale and preregistered evidence. Owen and colleagues' six-week online study included 11,430 participants: practiced tasks improved, but transfer to untrained tasks was not detected [66]. This is a large study, not claimed here to be preregistered. Zhong and colleagues' later preregistered double-blind study also found greater training gains with longer training but no near or far transfer; its opened abstract did not report sample size, so it is not labeled a large replication [67]. Together these substantially weaken the classic general-intelligence interpretation of commercial working-memory training.

Expert mnemonic performance is not imaginary. Dresler and colleagues showed that six weeks of mnemonic training could move novices' functional-network patterns toward those of memory athletes, with memory improvement associated with those patterns up to four months later [68]. A meta-analysis of 21 randomized mnemonic-training studies and 4,149 healthy older adults found immediate episodic-memory g = 0.408 and long-term g = 0.418 [69]. These are durable benefits from strategy and practice, not a transient whole-brain upgrade. They apply most directly when material can be organized with the learned strategy; reasoning and attention outside that context are not thereby proven to improve.

Mindfulness also has mixed rather than uniformly null evidence. A meta-analysis of 111 RCTs and 9,538 participants reported active-control effects ranging from g = 0.192 to 0.394 for the outcomes that benefited. Episodic memory, processing speed and several latency measures did not improve; benefits were stronger in people with elevated psychiatric symptoms than in healthy controls [70]. The large registered Lenze trial described above found no significant cognitive benefit from mindfulness alone or combined with exercise [39]. The meta-analysis excluded single-session interventions, so it cannot establish an acute meditation-induced broad enhancement state.

Onset ranges from immediate use of a learned strategy to weeks, months or years of developing expertise. Large expertise advantages are often content- and representation-specific. Training changes are not deliberately reversible on demand. Profile assessment: robust routes to becoming better at selected things, not the requested large broad acute reversible state. Far-transfer brain-training claims are among the clearest dead ends in this evidence base; learning useful knowledge and strategies is not.

## Replication audit: what actually shrank or failed

The following distinctions prevent an exaggerated “everything failed replication” story.

Large, prospective or preregistered negative tests include the 241-person preregistered tDCS memory trial [4], the registered 585-person exercise/mindfulness factorial trial [39], the registered Testosterone Trials cognitive study [51], and the registered 413-person NOURISH AD study [45]. Their populations and interventions differ. Only the tDCS trial directly tests acute stimulation in both younger and cognitively normal older adults; none is a universal test of all enhancement.

Smaller preregistered or registered negatives include the theta-tACS working-memory test [9], the cervical-VNS learning replication [20], and the sham-controlled alpha-neurofeedback experiment [28]. They weaken specific mechanisms/protocols without supplying field-wide large-sample closure.

Large but not established here as preregistered: the 11,430-person brain-training study [66]. Preregistered but not established here as large: the later working-memory transfer study [67]. Small direct replication with physiology preserved but behavior absent: closed-loop acoustic sleep stimulation [24].

Meta-analytic shrinkage is another category: healthy exercise d = 0.22 to bias-adjusted d = 0.05 [37]; neurofeedback attention becoming nonsignificant against sham [26]; tACS performance showing publication-bias signals [5]; and VNS g = 0.41 to trim-and-fill g = 0.338 [18]. Statistical bias corrections are sensitivity analyses, not replacement randomized trials.

Retraction is stronger than ordinary shrinkage: the Stanyer acoustic-sleep meta-analysis is invalidated and must not support the verdict [23]. Creatine's double-counting critique and reanalysis history concern dependence of outcomes and exaggerated precision, not merely one failed experiment [49]. TMS hippocampal-network findings remain positive but have small studies, selective effects and a contested replication/reanalysis history [12].

For focused ultrasound, temporal interference, large healthy-person photobiomodulation effects, noninvasive BCI cognitive enhancement, acute hyperoxia's broad profile and controlled hypoxic/thermal enhancement, no decisive large preregistered independent replication was located. These claims are unconfirmed, not all disproven. Full reversibility of an enhancement state is also not established by this literature.

## Ranked leads closest to the requested profile

This ranking is a qualitative research judgment balancing profile fit and evidence, not a calculated probability of discovering broad improvement and not advice to use an intervention.

1. Precision network stimulation: tACS and hippocampal-network TMS. These offer actual human causal leverage over cognitive processing, sometimes acutely, with noninvasive delivery. The trade-off is severe: the healthy-young tACS average is small, and the stronger TMS memory effect is specifically not broad. Closed-loop personalization is a hypothesis for improving this trade-off, not a demonstrated escape from it.

2. Acute normobaric hyperoxia. Among the closest published timing/magnitude/breadth signals, including substantial domain estimates in healthy humans. It ranks below a validated enhancement because the underlying evidence is low quality and lacks decisive broad confirmatory replication. Its physiological risks and absence of demonstrated full reversibility prevent treating transient exposure as automatically innocuous.

3. Deep focused-ultrasound neuromodulation, with temporal interference as an adjacent emerging approach. Deep-circuit access and acute effects make these technically relevant to the original goal. Behavioral magnitude, breadth, independent replication and reversibility remain largely unestablished. This is a research-access route, not an available enhancement solution.

4. VNS paired with learning. It has a positive pooled cognitive signal and selective human learning demonstrations, but learning-specific effects, modest average magnitude, blinding/target-engagement uncertainty and null replications keep it well short of the target.

5. Photobiomodulation. Large published pooled estimates deserve attention. Mixed populations, heterogeneous protocols and absent large preregistered healthy-person confirmation make confidence much lower than the numbers alone suggest.

6. Ketone-related metabolic intervention. There is now a modest pooled signal including nominally healthy samples and some acute positive studies. High heterogeneity, inconsistent results and no demonstrated broad large response limit its fit.

7. Acute exercise and retinal light. Better-established modest state effects, relatively good timing, but the magnitude and cognitive breadth are plainly insufficient. Their ordinary health or alertness value is not a substitute answer to the original aim.

8. Sleep-targeted consolidation and mnemonic training. Worthwhile mechanisms for improving selected memories, with modest pooled effects and, for strategies, potentially large practiced-task gains. Wrong breadth, and usually wrong state/onset/reversibility profile for broad improvement.

Invasive closed-loop memory prostheses sit outside this practical ranking: they are a particularly strong proof of concept for targeted encoding assistance, but fail the healthy-person, accessibility and whole-intervention reversibility requirements. Neurofeedback has weaker specific sham-controlled evidence than its popularity suggests.

Current dead ends for the stated goal are generic brain-training far transfer, hormonal “optimization” in otherwise normal physiology, acute hypoxia, fasting as an acute cognitive amplifier, and heat/cold stress as a broad enhancer. “Dead end” here means not supported as a route to this requested capability, not useless for every clinical, fitness, learning or health objective.

For Rolf, the conclusion is not “settle for exercise instead.” It is that no verified non-drug method currently delivers the requested experience. The scientifically interesting routes require supervised human research access and stronger behavioral evidence; being an undergraduate without a wet lab does not prevent intellectual work on them, but it does not make the stimulation or implantation infrastructure unnecessary. There is also no verified basis here for adding individually small effects into a large multimodal stack.
